A Nostr identity is a secp256k1 keypair. Not mostly. Entirely.

Your npub *is* the public half of that keypair. [NIP-06](https://github.com/nostr-protocol/nips/blob/master/06.md) derives it from a mnemonic, relays index by it, every signature verifies against it, and every client treats it as the account. There is no envelope around it with room for other keys, no capability field, no version byte, nothing that says what else this identity can do.

That single fact is why post-quantum direct messages are opt-in, and it is worth being precise about, because "opt-in" usually implies somebody chose caution. Nobody chose. The protocol was designed around an identity that has no post-quantum half, and you cannot make a default out of something the identity layer cannot express.

## What post-quantum encryption actually needs

To send someone a post-quantum protected message you need their **ML-KEM public key**, a lattice key roughly 1,568 bytes long. It has no relationship to secp256k1. It is not a transformation of their npub, not a hash of it, not derivable from it by any means.

That is not an accident of our design, it is the point of it. Our keys are derived from the BIP-39 seed as *siblings* of the secp256k1 key, deliberately never *from* it. If a post-quantum key could be computed from a Nostr private key, then Shor's algorithm recovers the private key from the published pubkey, the attacker runs the same derivation, and the post-quantum key falls with it. The scheme would be circular and worth nothing.

So the property that makes the derivation safe is the same property that makes automatic use impossible. **A sender cannot look at an npub and learn anything about post-quantum capability, by construction.**

## The key has to be published separately, and usually is not

Because it cannot be derived, it has to be announced. A user publishes a `kind:10203` event carrying their ML-KEM and ML-DSA public keys, signed by their ordinary Nostr key, with a proof of possession binding the three together.

Two consequences follow, and both of them force opt-in.

**Most identities have no such event.** The overwhelming majority of Nostr accounts have never published one and many never will. A protocol default has to work for everyone; this cannot work for anyone who has not taken a separate action first.

**Finding out requires a network round trip.** The sender has to fetch the recipient's attestation from a relay, verify its proof of possession, and only then encrypt. That is a lookup with latency, a cache, and a failure mode, sitting in front of an operation callers expect to be immediate.

## Additive by necessity, optional by consequence

Put those together and the shape is forced. Post-quantum cannot replace anything, because the identity everything else depends on is still secp256k1. It can only be **added beside** what exists: an extra key, published in an extra event, used by an extra argument.

Anything purely additive is optional by definition. Something has to decide whether to use it, that something needs the recipient's key in hand, and the only layer holding both the key and the context is the application. So the call is explicit:

```js
window.nostr.nip44.encrypt(pubkey, plaintext, { scheme: 'pq', recipientKemKey })
```

Not because explicitness is a virtue. Because there is nowhere else the decision could live.

## The trap that comes with being additive

Additive has a cost, and it caught us during implementation.

JavaScript discards surplus arguments. A signer that has never heard of post-quantum receives that third argument, ignores it, encrypts the message the ordinary way, and returns a perfectly valid ciphertext. No error. Nothing in the return value distinguishes it from the post-quantum one.

A client that assumed support would then show the user a badge saying the message is quantum-protected. It is not. Someone who knows their messages are ordinary encrypted messages behaves accordingly; someone who has been told otherwise does not.

So the signer publishes what it accepts, and a client asks instead of guessing:

```js
window.nostr.nip44.schemes  // ["nip44", "pq"]
```

An absent `schemes` and a `schemes` of `["nip44"]` are different answers: the first is an older signer whose capability is unknown, the second is a signer stating it does not do post-quantum. Both mean do not send post-quantum, but only one of them is an answer.

This is worth stressing as a *consequence* rather than the reason. The capability marker exists because post-quantum had to be additive. Additive is not a style choice, it is what the identity layer permits.

## Decryption is not opt-in, and that asymmetry proves the point

Reading a post-quantum message requires no flag at all. The payload leads with a version byte and an algorithm byte, so the signer inspects it and routes it. A caller cannot get it wrong because it is never asked.

The difference is where the information lives. Coming in, the ciphertext knows what it is. Going out, nothing knows: not the npub, not the protocol, not the signer. Only the application, and only after it has fetched something.

**Infer when the data can tell you. Ask when it cannot.** Encryption is opt-in because the identity cannot tell you.

## What a user actually sees

Three states, not two.

| State | What holds |
| --- | --- |
| **Extra safe** | Messages are unreadable, nobody can see who you are talking to, and a future quantum computer could not open them |
| **Safe** | Messages are unreadable and nobody can see who you are talking to, but a quantum computer could open them one day |
| **Basic** | Messages are unreadable, but the servers carrying them can see who you are talking to |

Most conversations sit in the middle, and the middle is genuinely good news. [NIP-17](https://github.com/nostr-protocol/nips/blob/master/17.md) gift wrap already hides who is talking to whom, which is real protection working right now. Reducing this to quantum-safe or not says nothing about the protection a user does have, and turns the ordinary case into a permanent warning that people stop reading. [Obelisk](https://obelisk.ar) shows it as one small icon in the conversation header rather than a banner.

## What would stop it being opt-in

The honest answer is that opt-in is a symptom, and the cure is not on the messaging layer at all.

Post-quantum stops being additive when the **identity** stops being secp256k1-only: when a Nostr identity carries a post-quantum component the way it carries its curve key today, so that having an npub means having a post-quantum key, and a sender can stop asking.

That is a protocol-wide change. Signatures would move too, and an ML-DSA-87 signature is 4,627 bytes on every event, which is a relay storage and bandwidth decision that needs relay operators at the table rather than a client shipping unilaterally. It is not close, and it should not be rushed.

Until then, opt-in is not the conservative option. It is the only shape available, and pretending otherwise would mean a client promising protection the identity layer cannot deliver.

What can be done in advance is the half that expires: publishing the keys now, so messages sent today are protected against a break years away. That is [harvest now, decrypt later](https://obelisk.ar/guides/quantum-safe-dms), and it is the one part of this problem that cannot be fixed retroactively.

## The specifications

Public drafts, so a second implementation can interoperate without reading our source:

- [Draft 01: key derivation](https://github.com/nostr-wot/nostr-wot-extension/blob/main/nips/01-pq-key-derivation.md): siblings of the secp256k1 key, never derived from it, and why that direction is the whole security property
- [Draft 02: key attestation](https://github.com/nostr-wot/nostr-wot-extension/blob/main/nips/02-pq-key-attestation.md): the `kind:10203` event that exists because the key cannot be derived
- [Draft 03: the post-quantum NIP-44 envelope](https://github.com/nostr-wot/nostr-wot-extension/blob/main/nips/03-pq-nip44-envelope.md): the self-describing wire format that makes decryption inferable
- [Draft 04: signer encryption capability](https://github.com/nostr-wot/nostr-wot-extension/blob/main/nips/04-nip07-encryption-capability.md): `schemes`, and the downgrade it prevents

None of them has a NIP number yet. Disagreement about the wire format is more useful now than after a second client ships against it.

The identity half is covered in [post-quantum Nostr identities](/blog/post-quantum-nostr-identities), and the implementation write-up is on [QuantaKrypto](https://quantakrypto.com/blog/obelisk-first-nostr-client-post-quantum-dms).
